# Electron scattering at etched graphene edges, and methodology/validation of semiclassical event-driven Monte Carlo transport simulators

Scope note: this file covers (A) what is known about boundary (edge) scattering of electrons in etched graphene and the ballistic/hydrodynamic reference results for constrictions, and (B) the methodology, contact-injection model, analytic acceptance tests, GPU implementation issues, and validity limits of semiclassical event-driven Monte Carlo (MC) / ray-tracing transport codes. Numbers marked "[computed here]" were calculated by the researcher (formulas stated) and are not literature values; everything else carries an inline source. Items marked "[single paper]" or "[unverified]" should be treated accordingly.

## A1. What specularity / diffuse fraction has been inferred for etched graphene edges, and by what method?

### Takeaway
There is no consensus single number. Magnetic-focusing experiments on oxygen-plasma-etched hBN/graphene edges give an edge "specularity" (second-to-first focusing-peak amplitude ratio) of 0.2–0.5 (Taychatanapat et al. 2013), whereas boundary-magnetoresistance peaks in 1-µm graphene wires (Masubuchi et al. 2012) and ray-tracing fits to scanning-gate images of cyclotron orbits (Bhandari et al. 2016) are consistent with nearly fully diffuse (p ≈ 0) reactive-ion-etched edges. A defensible default for an RIE-etched graphene edge is therefore "mostly diffuse, with a specular fraction p in the range 0–0.5, p ≈ 0.2–0.3 as a central estimate", and p should be treated as a sensitivity parameter.

### Cited Findings
- Masubuchi et al. (PRL 109, 036601 (2012); arXiv:1205.5150) measured magnetoresistance of ballistic graphene/hBN wires with W = 1.0 µm (L = 0.6, 1.4, 2.3 µm) and (L,W) = (3.1, 1.5), (2.5, 0.95), (1.4, 1.0) µm; hole mobility at 4 K was 63,000 cm²/Vs, long-range-limited mean free path l_long ≈ 1.2 µm and short-range l_short ≈ 2 µm at V_g = −45 V, both exceeding the 1 µm wire width. — [arXiv:1205.5150](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.5150)
- Same paper: the characteristic magnetoresistance peak appears at W/R_c = 0.9 ± 0.1 (for both W = 1.0 and 1.5 µm devices), compared with W/R_c = 0.55 for a classical semiconductor 2DES (Thornton et al.); the peaks are attributed to diffuse boundary scattering, and the authors state that "nearly zero specularity at the graphene boundary is suggested" (i.e., p ≈ 0), because the ΔR amplitude emerges when l_long ≈ W. No numerical p was fitted; the framework cited is Thornton et al. and Beenakker–van Houten. The anomalous W/R_c is tentatively attributed to a Dirac-electron wavefunction phase shift upon boundary scattering (Rakyta et al.) and compared with first-principles nanoribbon results W/R_c ≈ 0.8–1.0 (Xu and Zozoulenko); the authors say "the exact reason still needs further work". Boundary-scattering ΔR remained measurable to 240 K. — [arXiv:1205.5150](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.5150)
- Taychatanapat et al. (Nat. Phys. 9, 225 (2013); arXiv:1301.1969): transverse magnetic focusing (TMF) in oxygen-plasma-etched hBN-supported Hall bars with injector–collector spacing L = 500 nm (monolayer), 775 nm (bilayer), 950 nm (trilayer), contact widths ≈100 nm (MLG, BLG) and ≈240 nm (TLG); field-effect mobility ≈100,000 cm²/Vs at low T (mean free path ≈1 µm). Three focusing peaks (orders p = 1, 2, 3) are visible for |B| < 2.5 T in MLG; the first TMF mode survives at 300 K in MLG. — [arXiv:1301.1969](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.1969)
- Same paper defines edge "specularity" as the ratio of second-mode to first-mode focusing-peak amplitude and reports "the value of specularity ranges from 0.2 to 0.5" across devices, stating that "a significant fraction of the electrons get specularly reflected off the graphene edge"; for comparison, focused-ion-beam-etched semiconductor edges gave values below 1 and electrostatically defined edges gave values above 1. Quantum-interference fine structure on the peaks washes out at ≈15 K; MLG peak amplitude decreases linearly with T, attributed to longitudinal-acoustic-phonon scattering. — [arXiv:1301.1969](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.1969)
- Kiselev & Schmalian (PRB 99, 035430 (2019)) cite this measured specularity q = 0.2–0.5 for oxygen-plasma-etched graphene as placing those edges between the fully specular and fully diffuse limits of their slip-length theory. — [arXiv:1806.03933](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.03933)
- Bhandari, Lee, Kim, Westervelt (J. Electron. Mater. 2017; arXiv:1608.02248) fitted cooled-scanning-probe images of cyclotron orbits in an RIE-etched hBN/graphene/hBN Hall bar (700-nm-wide contacts, 2.0 µm apart, 285 nm SiO₂ back gate; T = 4.2 K, n = 1.13×10¹² cm⁻², B = 0.12–0.14 T) with classical ray tracing (N = 10,000 trajectories, cosine-law injection, Lorentz force + tip force). They conclude: "We find that the scattering of electrons from the graphene edge is diffusive"; the edge was modelled with a cosine-law (Lambertian) re-emission, P(θ) = (1/2)cos θ; trajectories that would produce the second focusing peak "are diffusively scattered by edge roughness". They note the edge "is patterned by a Reactive Ion Etcher (RIE)". — [arXiv:1608.02248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.02248) (full text extracted from the PDF)
- A 2019 TMF paper on Fermi-surface anisotropy summarizes the conflict: Taychatanapat et al., observing higher-order focusing peaks, found that edge reflections are specular, while Masubuchi et al. reported that reflections are diffusive from nanowire edge-scattering experiments. — [arXiv:1901.06073](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.06073)
- Chin. Phys. Lett. 39, 097302 (2022), BN-encapsulated graphene: boundary-scattering negative magnetoresistance survives down to a ballistic ratio L_mfp/W as low as 0.15 (vs the usual expectation L_mfp/W ≥ 1), attributed to "the ultra-low specularity of the sample edge of our graphene devices"; no numerical p is given in the abstract. — [CPL 2022](https://cpl.iphy.ac.cn/article/doi/10.1088/0256-307X/39/9/097302)
- Auton et al. (Nat. Commun. 2016, graphene ballistic nano-rectifier, hBN-encapsulated, 122-nm source and 137-nm drain leads by AFM) note that "It is likely that the ballistically conducting region of the graphene does not extend all the way to the edge", invoking edge states and traps; the 50 K conductance oscillations were analyzed with lateral quantization k⊥W = πm giving an average peak spacing of 15.1 meV. — [PMC4895026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4895026/)
- Terrés et al. (Nat. Commun. 7, 11528 (2016)) found that in etched constrictions (W ≈ 230–850 nm) the mismatch between the width inferred from conductance and the SEM width is explained by "reflections at the rough edges of the constriction and not a reduction in active channel width"; their tight-binding simulations used an edge-roughness amplitude ΔW = 0.2W, and for the smallest constriction a transmission-reduction factor c₀ ≈ 0.56. — [PMC4876454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4876454/)
- Geurs et al. (graphene Tesla valve, 2020) remark that "Narrower channels are influenced more by edge roughness, which destroys momentum conservation in the electron fluid"; no specularity value given. — [arXiv:2008.04862](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.04862v1)

### Inferences
- The two experimental "specularity" numbers measure different things: Taychatanapat's 0.2–0.5 is a ratio of focusing-peak amplitudes (a proxy that also contains the spatial-smearing of a diffusely-reflected beam, dephasing and bulk scattering along a path twice as long), so it is an upper bound on the true single-bounce specular probability p rather than p itself. Masubuchi's and Bhandari's "p ≈ 0 / diffuse" conclusions come from fitting boundary-dominated features and are consistent with p being small (≲0.3).
- For an RIE/O₂-plasma-etched graphene edge in hBN, a simulator should default to a mostly diffuse model (Lambertian re-emission) with an optional specular fraction p ∈ [0, 0.5]; p = 0 (fully diffuse) and p = 1 (fully specular) bracket the physics and should both be run as sensitivity cases.
- The anomalous W/R_c = 0.9 (vs 0.55 for 2DEG) in Masubuchi's data is a warning that a purely classical diffuse-wall billiard may not reproduce the detailed position of boundary-magnetoresistance features in graphene even when the diffuse assumption is right; this is a candidate quantum (phase-shift) correction and should not be used as a hard acceptance test.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that reports a fitted Fuchs-type specularity p for graphene with a stated uncertainty; all values are proxies (peak ratios) or qualitative (p ≈ 0).
- The Masubuchi PRL full text was obtained only via the arXiv v1 HTML; the published version could differ in details.
- No dependence of specularity on edge-fabrication method (RIE vs. graphite-cleaved edge vs. electrostatic/gate-defined edge vs. hBN-trench) was found quantified for graphene, except Chen et al.'s expectation that a cleaved-graphite-defined junction is smooth (see A2/B6).

## A2. Theory of boundary scattering in graphene (edge orientation, roughness, intervalley scattering, Berry phase) and models used in semiclassical simulation; are etched edges predominantly diffuse?

### Takeaway
Theory supports the view that realistic etched graphene edges are diffuse: Walter et al. (PRL 2018) showed that disordered edge modes at a graphene boundary produce resonant scattering and diffusive reflection even when λ_F ≫ disorder correlation length, which violates the usual (Ziman/Soffer) expectation that long-wavelength electrons see a rough edge as smooth. Standard semiclassical models in use are: a fixed Fuchs specularity p (probability p of specular reflection, 1−p of Lambertian/cosine-law re-emission), the angle-dependent Ziman/Soffer form p(θ) = exp[−(4π h cos θ/λ)²] (valid only for small roughness, Kirchhoff limit), and fully diffuse cosine-law walls (used by Bhandari et al.). Pure classical billiards used by experimentalists for graphene (Chen 2016; Auton 2016; the Z-diode PIC-MC) mostly assume specular edges, which is the less physical limit for etched edges.

### Cited Findings
- Walter, Rosdahl, Akhmerov, Hassler (PRL 121, 136803 (2018)): "resonant scattering off disordered edge modes results in diffusive electron reflection even when the electron wavelength is much longer than the disorder correlation length"; linearly dispersing edge states "turn into disorder-broadened bands" and when these overlap E = 0 (relative to the edge potential) they act as the resonant scatterer; the condition is roughly s_d ≳ |V_d − E_F| where V_d is the mean edge-band energy and s_d the disorder strength. Off resonance (|V_d − E_F| ≳ s_d) the reflection-angle variance grows quadratically with disorder and decays with λ_F (specular, as in the law of reflection); on resonance it "increases linearly with s_d, independent of the Fermi wavelength". Tight-binding (which includes intervalley scattering) agrees "with the single valley prediction of the Dirac equation up to numerical prefactors" (Var_tb ≈ 0.1 Var_Dirac). Analysis is for a zigzag edge but the authors "expect our results to apply to an arbitrary termination direction". Predicted signature: a dip in nonlocal conductance at the second magnetic-focusing resonance, B_n = 2nE_F/(e v_F W_x), when a side gate tunes the edge band onto E_F; example simulation parameters N = 6 disordered rows, V_d = 0.062 eV, s_d = 0.047 eV, E_F = 0.093 eV, disorder extent ≈2.1a ≪ λ_F ≈ 18a. — [arXiv:1804.04142](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.04142)
- Rakyta, Kormányos, Cserti, Koskinen (PRB 81, 115411 (2010)) combined semiclassical and tight-binding calculations of coherent electron focusing in graphene nanoribbons: armchair edges give equidistant focusing peaks; zigzag edges show focusing peaks at low fields but a more complex interference structure at higher fields; they propose TMF as an edge-structure probe. — [PRB 81, 115411](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevB.81.115411)
- Masubuchi et al. invoke Rakyta et al.: chiral Dirac carriers acquire a wavefunction phase shift when boundary scattering changes their momentum, causing interference, offered as an explanation of W/R_c = 0.9 instead of 0.55. — [arXiv:1205.5150](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.5150)
- Wurm, Richter, Adagideli, "Edge effects in graphene nanostructures: II. Semiclassical theory of spectral fluctuations and quantum transport" (2011) develops the semiclassical (trajectory-based) theory of graphene billiards including edge-induced intervalley scattering. — [arXiv:1108.1457](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.1457)
- Soffer (1967) is cited, alongside Berman et al. and Ziman, as a source of a momentum-dependent specularity parameter; the roughness-dependent specularity is usually written p = exp(−16π²η²cos²θ/λ²) (equivalently p = exp(−4η²k²cos²θ) with k = 2π/λ, η = rms roughness). Maznev shows this holds "only in the Kirchhoff approximation which assumes that the correlation length is large compared with the wavelength"; specularity-parameter models "fail when the surfaces are very rough, but appear to work well for small roughnesses". — [arXiv:1411.1721](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1721); [arXiv:1602.07735](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07735)
- Kiselev & Schmalian (PRB 99, 035430 (2019)) use two kinetic boundary models: (i) nearly specular, with a Fuchs-type angle-dependent specularity p(k) = 1 − 4k_y∫(dk'_x/2π)k'_y W(k_x − k'_x) built from a Gaussian roughness correlation ⟨ξ(x)ξ(0)⟩ = h² exp(−x²/h'²) (h amplitude, h' correlation length), controlled by the small parameter h²h'/λ³; (ii) fully diffuse, where the outgoing distribution is direction-independent so "all tangential momentum is lost in an electron-wall collision". The resulting hydrodynamic slip length is ζ = f(κ)·l_ee with κ = h²h'/λ³; for Dirac electrons f₀ ≈ 0.008 (nearly specular, ζ ≈ 0.008 λ³/(h²h') · l_ee, parametrically ≫ l_ee) and diffuse ζ ≈ 0.6 l_ee; for a 2D Fermi liquid diffuse ζ ≈ 1.2 l_ee. For graphene with diffuse edges ζ "ranges from 100 µm at 1 K to 0.4 µm at 100 K"; the paper concludes that for graphene "it is more appropriate to assume no-stress than no-slip conditions" because ζ exceeds 1 µm below 100 K, and that "the ultimate low-temperature flow is always in the no-stress regime". — [arXiv:1806.03933](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.03933)
- Later work (PRB 108, 125305 (2023)) finds the magnetic-field dependence of the slip length follows an approximate theory well when the specular reflection probability is high, but for low specularity the slip length falls off faster with field once the cyclotron radius becomes comparable to l_ee. — [PRB 108, 125305](https://journals.aps.org/prb/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevB.108.125305); a 2026 preprint treats the Hall effect in viscous 2D electron flow "in samples with edges of arbitrary roughness". — [arXiv:2608.26979](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26979)
- Bhandari et al. implemented the diffuse edge as: reflection angle random "multiplied by the cosine of the angle of reflection", i.e., P(θ) = (1/2)cos θ (Lambert law), so "the majority of trajectories are reflected normal to the reflecting surface and very few get reflected parallel". They also note the gate-induced charge-density divergence at the edge (field ∝ 1/√distance for a semi-infinite plate over a ground plane) increases r_c near the edge and was not included. — [arXiv:1608.02248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.02248)
- Chen et al. (Science 2016, electron optics with ballistic graphene junctions) did not include junction/edge roughness; the pn junction was defined by a cleaved graphite edge and "expected to be smooth". — [arXiv:1602.08182](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.08182)
- The Z-shaped geometric-diode particle-in-cell MC (2021) uses "If a particle collides with a device boundary, it is reflected specularly and continues propagating" with no diffuse option. — [PMC8470659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8470659/)
- Drude-model simulations of graphene geometric diodes (Zhu et al., as cited by Mohebbi et al. 2024) also assume specular boundary reflections. — [DOAJ, Adv. Electron. Mater. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/4bd10bb4571448c382f1b1e72a72dbea)
- 1910.13737 (particle simulation of 2D Fermi-liquid ballistic flow): "The device boundaries specularly reflect incident quasiparticles". — [arXiv:1910.13737](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.13737)

### Inferences
- Because graphene's linear dispersion makes λ_F large (35 nm at 10¹² cm⁻², see B5) one might naively expect atomic-scale edge roughness (≲ 1 nm) to be invisible and edges to be specular; Walter et al. show this intuition fails in graphene because of edge states, so a diffuse default is theoretically defensible for disordered (etched) edges.
- The slip-length results map the boundary model onto a continuum quantity: fully diffuse edges ↔ slip length ≈ 0.6–1.2 l_ee; nearly specular edges ↔ slip length ≫ l_ee (effectively no-stress). A kinetic/MC code with p ≈ 0 therefore corresponds to the "no-slip-like" end only when W ≫ l_ee; in the ballistic regime (l_ee ≫ W) the distinction between diffuse-wall MC and hydrodynamic no-slip is moot because the hydrodynamic description itself does not apply.
- Angular dependence (Soffer/Ziman p(θ)) matters for grazing trajectories (cos θ → 0 → p → 1): a Tesla-valve-like channel with long straight walls has many grazing hits, so a constant p and a Soffer p(θ) with the same normal-incidence value can give noticeably different channel transmissions; this is an argument to implement p(θ) as an option and quote both.

### Gaps
- No full-text access was obtained to Rakyta et al. (PRB 2010) or Xu & Zozoulenko (nanoribbon W/R_c ≈ 0.8–1.0); their statements are reported second-hand via Masubuchi et al.
- No source was found that quantifies the diffuse fraction as a function of RIE edge roughness amplitude/correlation length specifically for graphene (Kiselev–Schmalian give the functional form but do not fit to measured roughness).
- Berry-phase/valley effects on the classical specular-reflection angle were not found quantified beyond the Masubuchi/Rakyta phase-shift argument.

## A3. Ballistic (Sharvin) and hydrodynamic reference results for graphene constrictions / point contacts

### Takeaway
The Sharvin/Landauer ballistic conductance of a graphene constriction of width w is G_ball = (4e²/h)(k_F w/π) = (2e²/h)·(4w/λ_F)/2 … i.e., g_sv e²/h × (k_F w/π) with spin–valley degeneracy g_sv = 4; this is the number every ballistic simulator must reproduce in the limit of specular (or no) walls and a short constriction. Guo–Levitov predict a viscous (Gurzhi) contribution G_vis = πn²e²w²/(32η) that adds to the ballistic conductance ("anti-Matthiessen", G = G_ball + G_vis), and Krishna Kumar et al. (Nat. Phys. 2017) observed conductance exceeding G_ball ("superballistic") below ≈150 K in hBN-encapsulated constrictions.

### Cited Findings
- Guo, Ilseven, Falkovich, Levitov (PNAS 2017): the ballistic model "predicts the conductance" G_ball = (2e²/h)N with N ≈ 2w/λ_F Landauer channels; their Eq. 35 gives R = (1/N)(h/e²)(λ_F/2w) with N the spin–valley degeneracy, "e.g. N = 4 for graphene", λ_F = 2π/k_F. Viscous conductance Eq. 1: G_vis(w) = πn²e²w²/(32η), valid for w ≫ l_ee; combination rule Eq. 3: G_VPC = G_ball + G_vis (additive in conductance, contrasted with Matthiessen's additive resistivity); crossover where R_vis = R_ball at l_ee(T)/w = π²/16 ≈ 0.62; viscosity η = ν n m = (1/4) v_F l_ee n m; influence function derived "with no-slip boundary conditions" (impenetrable no-slip boundary as the b → ∞ limit of a partially transparent boundary); diffuse boundary scattering not discussed. — [arXiv:1607.07269](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.07269)
- Krishna Kumar et al. (Nat. Phys. 13, 1182 (2017)): graphene constrictions whose conductance "below 150 K increases with increasing temperature"; "the measured conductance exceeds the maximum conductance possible for free electrons" (the Sharvin limit), attributed to collective electron motion which "shields individual carriers from momentum loss at sample boundaries"; the regime is "superballistic" because it goes beyond the Sharvin limit. — [arXiv:1703.06672](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06672); [Nature Physics DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/NPHYS4240)
- Terrés et al. (Nat. Commun. 2016): in etched graphene constrictions W ≈ 230–850 nm (reference Hall-bar mobility ≈150,000 cm²/Vs, mean free path > 1 µm), conductance shows evenly spaced kinks with ΔG typically 2–4 e²/h, one W ≈ 310 nm device showing steps close to 4e²/h; conductance "agrees excellently with the Landauer theory of ballistic transport at high carrier density"; ideally G rises by one quantum each time W·k_F reaches a multiple of π, with a factor of four for spin and valley; deviations from ideal behaviour appear for k_F below ≈50×10⁶ m⁻¹; sub-band spacing ΔE = 13.5 ± 2 meV; only kinks survive above ≈10 K; renormalized v_F ≈ 1.5×10⁶ m/s near neutrality. — [PMC4876454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4876454/)
- Bandurin et al. (Science 2016): negative local (vicinity) resistance near current injectors in doped graphene from "submicrometer-scale whirlpools"; electron viscosity ≈0.1 m²/s; hydrodynamic effects appear only when l_ee is the shortest length scale. — [arXiv:1509.04165](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.04165)
- Bandurin et al., "Fluidity onset in graphene" (Nat. Commun. 2018): l_ee = ħv_F ε_F/(k_BT)² (standard form; ħγ_ee ≃ T²/ε_F); device width 2.3 µm; regimes: free-particle ballistic (l_ee ≫ W), quasiballistic (x < l_ee < W, probe distance x ≈ 1–2 µm, Knudsen number Kn = l_ee/x > 1), hydrodynamic (l_ee ≪ x); the most negative vicinity resistance (fluidity onset) is at Kn = 1–3; at the R_v sign change at T = 20 K, l_ee ≈ 10 µm; at fluidity onset l_ee ≲ 0.2 µm; electron–phonon mean free path at 100 K, n = 10¹² cm⁻² ≈ 6 µm for monolayer and ≈2–4 µm for bilayer (Table 1). — [PMC6208423](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6208423/)
- Berdyugin et al. (Science 364, 162 (2019)): Hall viscosity measured via local voltages near current-injecting contacts in non-quantizing fields; effects persist to room temperature for devices smaller than ≈1 µm (press summary). — [arXiv:1806.01606](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01606); [Manchester news](https://www.manchester.ac.uk/discover/news/graphene-reveals-electrons-move-like-viscous-liquids/)
- Reference values [computed here] from G_ball = (4e²/h)(k_F w/π), k_F = √(πn), n = 10¹² cm⁻² (k_F = 1.772×10⁸ m⁻¹): w = 25 nm → N = k_F w/π = 1.41 modes, G = 219 µS (R = 4.6 kΩ); w = 50 nm → N = 2.82, G = 437 µS (2.29 kΩ); w = 100 nm → N = 5.64, G = 874 µS (1.14 kΩ); w = 200 nm → N = 11.3, G = 1.75 mS (572 Ω); w = 500 nm → N = 28.2, G = 4.37 mS (229 Ω); w = 1 µm → N = 56.4, G = 8.74 mS (114 Ω). Gurzhi crossover l_ee/w = π²/16 = 0.617.

### Inferences
- The classical MC analogue of G_ball is the flux of Fermi-surface carriers through an aperture of width w with cosine-law angular weighting; a code that injects with the flux-weighted (cos θ) distribution and counts transmitted particles will reproduce G_ball = (g_sv e²/h)(k_F w/π) exactly for an ideal aperture (derivation: G = g_sv (e²/h) ∫_{−π/2}^{π/2} (k_F w cos θ/2π) dθ = g_sv (e²/h) k_F w/π). This is the primary acceptance test for the contact and aperture code.
- Because the Sharvin value is independent of channel length only for specular walls, the ratio G(L)/G_ball for diffuse walls is the Clausing (Knudsen) factor of B3; the two tests together check both the injection distribution and the boundary model.
- The superballistic result is a hydrodynamic (e–e scattering) effect that a non-interacting MC cannot reproduce; a code that lacks e–e scattering should be expected to underestimate G in the 50–150 K window for constrictions with w ≳ l_ee, and this is an acknowledged limitation rather than a bug.

### Gaps
- The Krishna Kumar full text (constriction widths, extracted viscosities, percent excess over G_ball, contact-resistance subtraction) could not be read (PDF only; crude text extraction failed); only the abstract-level statements are reported.
- Numerical l_ee values at 200–300 K for monolayer graphene were not found explicitly; only the T⁻² formula and the 20 K (≈10 µm) and onset (≲0.2 µm) anchors.

## A4. Mean free path in hBN-encapsulated graphene vs temperature and density; relevance of e–e scattering length

### Takeaway
At cryogenic temperatures the momentum-relaxing mean free path in hBN-encapsulated graphene is limited by device size (≥ 1–3 µm, up to >28 µm in the best CVD samples); at room temperature it is phonon-limited to roughly 0.5–1 µm at n ≈ 10¹² cm⁻² (mobility ≈130,000–200,000 cm²/Vs). The e–e scattering length l_ee ∝ 1/T² falls from ≈10 µm at 20 K to ≲0.2 µm at the hydrodynamic onset (≈100–150 K), so for a sub-µm device at 100–300 K, l_ee can be shorter than both the device and the momentum-relaxing mean free path.

### Cited Findings
- Mayorov et al. (Nano Lett. 2011; arXiv:1103.4510): hBN-encapsulated graphene shows mean free path exceeding ≈3 µm at low T (mobility ≈500,000 cm²/Vs at n ≈ 10¹² cm⁻²) and "room temperature ballistic transport on a micrometer scale" (negative bend resistance over 1 µm). — [arXiv:1103.4510](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1103.4510)
- Wang et al. (Science 342, 614 (2013), 1D edge contacts): "low-temperature ballistic transport over distances longer than 15 micrometers, and room-temperature mobility comparable to the theoretical phonon-scattering limit". — [Columbia summary](https://bioee.ee.columbia.edu/one-dimensional-electrical-contact-to-a-two-dimensional-material/)
- Banszerus et al. (CVD graphene in hBN, 2016): mean free path "exceeds by far 28 µm" at 1.8 K (electron mobility >3×10⁶ cm²/Vs); cross device l_m ≥ 1 µm at 1.8 K with µ ≈ 147,000 cm²/Vs; ballistic (negative bend resistance) up to 200 K over 1 µm; phonon scattering "limits the room temperature mobility of graphene to values around 130,000 cm²/(Vs)" at ≈10¹² cm⁻² (citing Chen et al.; Hwang & Das Sarma); mean free path at 150/200/300 K shown in Fig. 2d is "comparable to 1 µm". Formulas used: l_m = (h/2e)µ√(n/π); l_m = σh/(2e²k_F) with k_F = √(πn); µ = 2e√π l_m/(h√n_th). — [arXiv:1511.08601](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1511.08601)
- Auton et al. (2016): "A mobility ∼200,000 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ is achieved at room temperature" (197,600 electrons / 106,000 holes from gating), with mean free path "longer than 1 µm at room temperature". — [PMC4895026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4895026/)
- Chen et al. (Science 2016): at room temperature "the graphene mean free path remains in excess of 1 µm"; the Veselago focusing signal vanishes at ≈70 K when the mean free path becomes comparable to the ≈7 µm resonant path length. — [arXiv:1602.08182](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.08182)

## B1. Standard semiclassical Monte Carlo methodology for graphene (Dirac dispersion, scattering rates, self-scattering, ensemble vs single-particle)

### Takeaway
The canonical reference for free-flight/self-scattering ensemble MC is Jacoboni & Reggiani (Rev. Mod. Phys. 55, 645 (1983)); graphene-specific ensemble MC codes (Chauhan & Guo 2009; Shishir & Ferry 2009; Fang, Konar, Xing, Jena PRB 2011; Rengel et al.; Coco et al. PRB 2021; Zalinyan & Nastasi PRB 2026) use the linear dispersion E = ħv_F k with acoustic (intravalley), optical and intervalley phonon rates, impurity scattering and, in some codes, e–e scattering; a key graphene peculiarity is that e–e collisions do not conserve ensemble velocity because of the linear dispersion.

### Cited Findings

- Chauhan & Guo, "High-field transport and velocity saturation in graphene", Appl. Phys. Lett. 95, 023120 (2009) — found only as a secondary citation [unverified content]. — [Doklady BSUIR reference list](https://doklady.bsuir.by/jour/article/view/903)
- Shishir & Ferry, "Velocity saturation in intrinsic graphene", J. Phys.: Condens. ### Inferences
- For a ballistic/quasi-ballistic device code (as opposed to a bulk-mobility code), the full phonon-rate machinery can be replaced in a first version by a single momentum-relaxing rate 1/τ = v_F/l_m with isotropic re-randomization (as in the PIC-MC above); this is exactly the "free flight + isotropic scattering" limit of Jacoboni–Reggiani and yields the Drude benchmark of B3. The phonon-resolved rates become necessary only when energy relaxation (hot carriers at high bias) matters.
- The non-conservation of ensemble velocity by e–e collisions in graphene means that adding a naive e–e scattering operator to an MC is not momentum-conserving and will not produce correct hydrodynamic (Gurzhi/superballistic) behaviour; reproducing A3's superballistic result requires a two-particle collision operator that conserves total momentum (expensive, cf. the sampled-partner approximation).

## B2. Correct injection of carriers from an ideal reservoir contact into a 2D ballistic region, and how to get I(V) from a Monte Carlo with reservoirs at µ_S and µ_D

### Takeaway
The established MC contact model treats the contact as a thermal reservoir: carriers are injected at a rate equal to the incoming flux density ζ_k f(ε_k) (velocity-weighted times Fermi–Dirac), carriers reaching a contact are absorbed and thermalized (no correlation), and Pauli exclusion is enforced at injection by a binomial/rejection scheme per k-cell. In 2D the angular distribution of injected carriers is the flux-weighted (Lambertian) P(θ) = ½cos θ, which the particle-simulation literature uses explicitly. Current is the net count of charge crossing the drain minus source; in linear response only the bias window (f_S − f_D) carries net current, so weighting injected particles by (f_S − f_D) (and injecting only in that window) is the variance-optimal, Landauer-consistent approach.

### Cited Findings
- González, Bulashenko, Mateos, Pardo, Reggiani et al. (MC of ballistic conductors, 1999/2001): "the density of incoming electron states with wave vector k impinging per unit time" ζ_k = n_k v_k, injection rate density Γ_k = ζ_k f(ε_k) with f the Fermi–Dirac function; "the electron injection from the thermal reservoirs is modeled according to Fermi statistics"; occupancy of each incoming k-state is a binomial random variable with success probability f(ε_k); implementation splits k-space into ≈50 meshes spanning ±3k_BT around ε_F, states outside treated as full/empty; in the degenerate limit an electron is injected every 1/ζ_k per mesh with no rejection; nondegenerate limit is a Poisson process with Maxwell–Boltzmann k; saturation current I_S = q∫₀^∞ v_k n_k f(ε_k) dk; carriers reaching a contact are "thermalized immediately, thus any correlation is destroyed"; checks: classical S_I(0) = 4qI_S and equilibrium S_I(0) = 8q²k_BT/h giving G = 2q²/h for a single 1D mode; "Coulomb interactions are ignored and Pauli exclusion principle is taken into account only at the contact injection"; the scheme "can be extended to two and three dimensions, and multi-subband systems". — [arXiv:cond-mat/9910125](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/9910125); [arXiv:cond-mat/0201181](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0201181)
- Particle simulation of 2D ballistic Fermi-liquid flow (1910.13737, supplement): each source gridpoint injects particles "with velocity v_F, at discrete angles θ_j" sampling [−π/2, π/2] with weight P(θ) = 0.5 cos(θ), "the angular distribution of injected particles", which corresponds to the shifted Fermi–Dirac f(µ + p·v_d) imposed at the source in the companion Boltzmann solver; current density is accumulated along trajectories, J(x) = Σ_i Σ_j Σ_n P(θ_j) v_ij(nΔt) δ(x − x_ij(nΔt)); each trajectory exits at the source or drain; "The currents are in good agreement, albeit with pronounced noise in the particle simulation" relative to the deterministic Boltzmann solver; resistance in the Landauer–Büttiker picture scales as (1 − t)/t. — [arXiv:1910.13737](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.13737)
- Bhandari et al.: "The trajectories are injected from a source with cosine angular distribution that follows from the foreshortening of the apparent contact width"; point sources every 50 nm across a 700 nm contact, N = 10,000; transmission T computed "by counting the fraction of emitted trajectories that reach the receiving contact". — [arXiv:1608.02248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.02248)
- Chen et al. (Science 2016) billiard model: "electrons are injected from the source at randomly distributed angles", weighted by a normal distribution with σ_inj = π/15; "Various σ_inj were also tested in our simulation but no dependence was found"; pn-junction transmission T ∼ exp(−πk_F d sin²θ) (generalized for asymmetric bias). — [arXiv:1602.08182](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.08182)
- Geometric-diode PIC-MC: when a carrier crosses an electrode it is counted and "another carrier is injected from the opposite electrode" (fixed particle number scheme), all at E_F. — [PMC8470659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8470659/)
- Landauer form used by the graphene constriction experiments: G = (4e²/h)·(number of modes), modes added each time k_F W crosses a multiple of π (Terrés et al.). — [PMC4876454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4876454/); G_ball = (g_sv e²/h)(k_F w/π) (Guo–Levitov Eq. 35). — [arXiv:1607.07269](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.07269)
- Bulashenko & Rubí (PRB 64, 045307): degenerate Fermi–Dirac injection into ballistic conductors suppresses shot noise via Fermi and Coulomb correlations (relevant if noise is to be computed). — [arXiv:cond-mat/0006499](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0006499)

### Inferences (derivation; standard kinetic theory, consistent with the cited sources)
- Injection flux for 2D graphene through a contact segment of length W (per spin/valley channel g_sv = 4): the number of carriers per unit time entering with |k| ∈ [k, k+dk], angle θ to the contact normal, is dΦ = g_sv · W · v_F cos θ · f(E − µ) · (k dk dθ)/(2π)². Integrating over θ gives the ½cos θ angular law (flux weighting), and over k the energy weighting ∝ k f(E) = (E/ħv_F) f(E): the injected energy distribution is the graphene DOS (∝ E) times Fermi–Dirac times v_F (constant), so the energy-sampling PDF is p(E) ∝ E f(E − µ). Carriers are injected uniformly along the contact length (Bhandari used equally spaced point sources).
- Net current: I = e[Φ_S→D − Φ_D→S]. Writing the single-particle transmission from S to D at energy E and angle θ as T(E, θ) (which in a billiard depends only on geometry and the boundary model), I = (g_sv e/h) ∫dE [f_S(E) − f_D(E)] ∫(k W cos θ/2π) T(E,θ) dθ — the Landauer formula with the mode sum replaced by the flux-weighted angular integral. This gives two equivalent MC estimators: (i) "two-reservoir" — inject from both contacts with weights f_S and f_D and take the difference of transmitted counts (statistically noisy because two large numbers are subtracted); (ii) "bias-window" — inject only with weight (f_S − f_D) ≥ 0 from S (and the negative part from D if E_F differs), so every particle contributes to net current. At zero bias estimator (ii) gives exactly zero (detailed balance), whereas estimator (i) gives zero only within statistical error — this is the detailed-balance acceptance test of B3. In the linear, low-T limit (ii) reduces to injecting Fermi-surface carriers (|k| = k_F) with weight eV, which is exactly what the 1910.13737 and PIC-MC codes do.
- Time-reversal/geometry check: for a non-interacting billiard without magnetic field, T_{S→D}(E) = T_{D→S}(E) for the total (angle-integrated) transmission, so the linear-response I(V) is antisymmetric and no rectification can appear at linear order; any rectification in an MC must come from (a) energy dependence of T over the bias window (|E_F ± eV/2| changes k_F, hence λ_F, hence nothing in a purely classical billiard with hard walls — but it does change the Fermi velocity direction distribution only through DOS weighting), (b) self-consistent electrostatics (Poisson), or (c) inelastic/momentum-relaxing scattering with non-reciprocal geometry. A classical hard-wall billiard with specular walls, energy-independent trajectories and no Poisson update cannot rectify; the geometric-diode literature therefore always includes either momentum relaxation (PIC-MC above: asymmetry → 1 as λ_MFP → 0, peaking at λ_MFP ≈ 6× neck width) or self-consistent fields. This is a key design consideration, not a literature statement: it follows from the Landauer formula for non-interacting carriers [researcher's inference].
- Contacts as ideal reservoirs implies no reflection at the contact plane; the Auton and Terrés results (ballistic region not extending to the edge; effective-width reduction c₀ ≈ 0.56) suggest adding a reflecting "dead" edge strip or a reduced effective width as a tunable correction.

### Gaps
- No source was found that states the 2D graphene energy-weighted injection PDF ∝ E f(E) explicitly; it is a direct derivation from ζ_k = n_k v_k in 2D with the Dirac DOS.
- Truccolo et al.'s detailed MC + Landauer–Büttiker implementation (how transmission matrices are assembled from trajectory counts) was not accessible (abstract only).

## B3. Analytic benchmarks for acceptance tests (Sharvin/Landauer, Clausing/Knudsen transmission, Drude, Matthiessen, detailed balance, current conservation)

### Takeaway
A compact acceptance suite is: (1) Sharvin conductance of an ideal aperture, G = (4e²/h)k_F W/π (A3); (2) transmission of a straight 2D channel of aspect ratio L/W — exactly 1 for specular walls, and the 2D Clausing factor for fully diffuse (Lambertian) walls (values computed here: K(L/W) = 0.890, 0.804, 0.683, 0.541, 0.400, 0.274, 0.179, 0.110 for L/W = 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, ±0.001; the same MC reproduced the classical circular-tube Clausing value 0.5134 at L/D = 1); (3) Drude limit σ = (2e²/h)k_F l for L ≫ l with isotropic scattering; (4) Matthiessen-type combination of bulk and wall scattering (approximate, with a logarithmic 2D Knudsen correction); (5) exactly zero net current at zero bias with symmetric injection; (6) particle/current conservation (sum of absorbed = injected). With Fuchs p ∈ (0,1) the result must lie monotonically between these.
- Matthiessen test: in a long channel (L ≫ W) with bulk mean free path l and diffuse walls, the effective mean free path l_eff obeys approximately 1/l_eff ≈ 1/l + 1/l_wall with l_wall of order W·ln(l/W) in 2D (log correction from grazing trajectories); exact Matthiessen additivity does not hold for boundary scattering, so the acceptance criterion should be "within ≈10–20% of Matthiessen" plus correct limits (l_eff → l for W → ∞, l_eff → l_wall for l → ∞) [researcher's inference; see Gaps].

Current conservation: the number of particles absorbed at all contacts plus those still in flight equals the number injected (event-driven codes should assert this every step).

## B4. GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo / ray-tracing transport codes: existing implementations, RNG choice, warp divergence and mitigation

### Takeaway
GPU ensemble MC for semiconductors exists (Hull GaN/III-V CUDA EMC thesis; Cooke & Ancona electromagnetic EMC 2023; Suzuki EMC/MD 2015) but no open-source GPU graphene MC was found. The dominant performance problem for event-driven particle loops ("one history per thread") is warp divergence from the lifetime tail: in a recent optical-photon ray-tracing benchmark only 2.6 of 32 lanes were active (8.3%) until Shader Execution Reordering raised it to 20.4 lanes (64%), giving a ≈15× wall-clock speedup; event-based (sorted/compacted) processing in neutron codes gives 2.6–6×. cuRAND Philox4x32-10 (counter-based, one subsequence per particle keyed by particle index) is the recommended choice for reproducibility under thread reordering; XORWOW (default) has documented seed-dependent statistical weaknesses in seeded ordering; MRG32k3a passes BigCrush with occasional marginal results.

- GPU radiation-transport MC: GPUMCD (dose calculation), fast GPU proton MC — "one history per thread" is the common paradigm, with "thread divergence that is inevitable", vs "one step per thread" kernels that are nearly divergence-free but memory-heavy; stream compaction (run fixed steps, remove finished particles, restart) has non-zero cost so "it is not clear if this technique will have a positive effect on execution time". — [arXiv:1101.1245](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1101.1245); [arXiv:1409.8336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.8336)

- Shader Execution Reordering study (optical photon MC, RTX 4090, 2026 preprint, single author/benchmark): "Short-lived photons leave inactive lanes while a few long-lived photons delay warp completion"; warp execution efficiency 2.64/32 lanes (8.3%) → 20.37/32 (63.7%) with SER; achieved occupancy 8.0% → 32.3%; kernel speedup 16.55× (wall 14.72×) with reorder every 4 bounces (15.2× every bounce, 15.3× every 16th); bit-identical hits; energy per event 2246 J → 237 J; comparison numbers cited: event-based OpenMC ≈6× faster than history-based on GPU; Shift 2.6–5.6× from event-based restructuring; IceCube per-warp shared-memory queues refill finished lanes (13.7 → 17.0 lanes). RNG: "cuRAND Philox4x32-10, with one subsequence per photon selected by its global photon index", making output independent of thread scheduling. Recommendations: single megakernel for trace + physics; reorder every few bounces; include a coherence key (current volume); verify bit-identical results with/without reordering. — [arXiv:2608.21396](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2608.21396)
- cuRAND host-API overview: XORWOW is the default ("xor-shift family"), "takes some time to setup the first time", and CURAND_ORDERING_PSEUDO_SEEDED "may result in statistical weaknesses of the pseudorandom output for some user seed values"; MRG32k3a is a combined multiple recursive generator; Philox4_32_10 is one of three counter-based RNGs from D. E. Shaw Research (SC11), 65536 distinct sequences in LEGACY host ordering, most efficient when generating a multiple of 4× threads; skip-ahead (offset) available for all except MTGP32 and MT19937. — [cuRAND docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/curand/host-api-overview.html)
- cuRAND testing: MRG32k3a passes all BigCrush tests "frequently, with occasional marginal results" (one WeightDistrib p = 5.2×10⁻⁴ in the shown run); Mersenne Twister consistently fails two linear-complexity tests. — [cuRAND testing](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/archive/12.6.1/curand/testing.html)
- rocRAND (AMD equivalent) documents MRG32k3a as 131072 generators separated by 2⁷⁶ values and XORWOW substreams offset by 2⁶⁷ values; Philox 4x32-10 is a single counter-based generator with differently handled substreams. — [rocRAND guide](https://rocm.docs.amd.com/projects/rocRAND/en/docs-6.2.0/conceptual/programmers-guide.html)
- Informal NVIDIA-forum benchmark: Philox fastest in a small test but state memory ≈64 MB vs 8 MB for a custom MLCG at 10⁶ states (anecdotal, unreviewed). — [NVIDIA forums](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/quick-benchmark-comparison-of-different-parallel-random-number-generators/40591)
- GPU photon-transport codes (MCX) show the mature "massively parallel GPU MC" pattern for 3D media but are for photons, not electrons/phonons. — [MCX README](https://pt.osdn.net/projects/sfnet_mcx/downloads/README.md)
- 2D ballistic particle methods have "a much higher noise floor" than deterministic Boltzmann solvers (1910.13737). — [arXiv:1910.13737](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.13737)

### Inferences
- For a 2D graphene billiard, per-particle work is tiny (segment–segment intersection and a reflection), so the loop is latency/occupancy-bound, not compute-bound — exactly the regime where the lifetime tail dominates: a few particles trapped in long diffuse-wall channels (the Tesla-valve loops) will keep warps resident. Mitigations in order of implementation cost: (a) cap the number of bounces/path length (l_m already provides a physical cap via momentum relaxation), (b) periodic stream compaction/respawn every k events, (c) persistent-thread work queues, (d) SER on Ada/Hopper-class hardware.
- Use a counter-based RNG (Philox4x32-10) keyed by particle index so results are bitwise reproducible regardless of thread scheduling or compaction — this also makes regression tests (B3) deterministic.
- Since the per-trajectory transmission result is a Bernoulli variable, the relative error of a transmission estimate is √((1−T)/(TN)); for T ≈ 0.1 (long diffuse channels) and 1% precision one needs N ≈ 10⁵ particles per bias/energy point — trivial on a GPU but worth stating in the test plan.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed open-source GPU electron MC for 2D materials was identified; no phonon-ray-tracing GPU code was retrieved despite searching (only photon codes).
- cuRAND documentation pages fetched did not state generator periods; the commonly quoted periods (XORWOW ≈2¹⁹², MRG32k3a ≈2¹⁹¹, Philox 2¹²⁸ counter × 2⁶⁴ key) were not verified from a fetched source.

## B5. Where does the semiclassical picture fail? (λ_F vs neck width, mode quantization, coherence length vs T, Klein tunnelling, what tight-binding/Kwant found)

### Takeaway
λ_F = 2π/√(πn) = 35 nm at n = 10¹² cm⁻² (50 nm at 5×10¹¹, 25 nm at 2×10¹², 11 nm at 10¹³) [computed here]; a 25 nm neck at 10¹² cm⁻² carries N = k_F W/π ≈ 1.4 modes, so transport through it is in the quantum (few-mode) regime where Landauer/tight-binding is required; the single-mode energy spacing ΔE ≈ πħv_F/W ≈ 83 meV for W = 25 nm exceeds k_BT at 300 K (26 meV) [computed here], so mode quantization is not thermally washed out even at room temperature. Experimentally, size quantization in etched constrictions is seen only for k_F ≳ 5×10⁷ m⁻¹ and T ≲ 10 K (Terrés), and Fabry–Pérot/phase-coherent interference in graphene has been reported up to ≈60 K (Young & Kim) with fine structure on TMF peaks gone by ≈15 K (Taychatanapat); no room-temperature phase-coherent interference in graphene devices was found. Large-scale TB/NEGF (Calogero et al., 100 nm flakes, 4×10⁵ atoms) reproduces semiclassical Veselago-dot caustics "in reasonable agreement" but shows interference broadening and states diffraction matters for apertures smaller than λ_F.

### Cited Findings
- Fermi wavelength and mode count [computed here]: k_F = √(πn); λ_F = 2π/k_F; E_F = ħv_F k_F with v_F = 10⁶ m/s. n = 10¹¹: λ_F = 112 nm, E_F = 37 meV; 5×10¹¹: 50 nm, 82 meV; 10¹²: 35.4 nm, 117 meV (k_BT(300 K)/E_F = 0.22); 2×10¹²: 25 nm, 165 meV; 5×10¹²: 16 nm, 261 meV; 10¹³: 11 nm, 369 meV. Modes N = k_F W/π at 10¹² cm⁻²: W = 25 nm → 1.4; 50 nm → 2.8; 100 nm → 5.6; 200 nm → 11.3. Thermal length L_T = ħv_F/k_BT ≈ 25 nm at 300 K, 1.9 µm at 4 K. Formula sources: λ_F = 2π/k_F (Guo–Levitov) — [arXiv:1607.07269](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.07269); k_F = √(πn) (Banszerus) — [arXiv:1511.08601](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1511.08601).
- Terrés et al.: size quantization (kinks spaced 2–4 e²/h) in etched constrictions W ≈ 230–850 nm; deviations from ideal Landauer behaviour for k_F < ≈50×10⁶ m⁻¹ (λ_F > ≈125 nm); "Above around 10 K only kinks related to quantized conductance survive"; TB simulations with ΔW = 0.2W edge roughness explain the smoothing; sub-band spacing ΔE = 13.5 ± 2 meV. — [PMC4876454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4876454/)
- Auton et al.: 50 K (and 150 K) conductance oscillations in a ≈120–140 nm-lead graphene rectifier analyzed with lateral quantization k⊥W = πm, ΔE = ħv_Fπ/W, average spacing 15.1 meV; "One prominent feature that the ballistic model does not explain is the oscillations at 50 K". — [PMC4895026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4895026/)
- Calogero et al. (large-scale TB+NEGF with TBtrans/sisl, benchmarked against Kwant with "good agreement"): 100 nm × 100 nm flake, 395,940 sites, 2-nm zigzag ribbon as point source, ≈1 min per energy point on 24 threads; Veselago-dot caustics "in reasonable agreement" with semiclassical results of Bøggild et al., especially the main cusp position; quantum interference broadens the beam; "quantum effects such as coherence are not naturally included in semiclassical simulations"; "chiral tunneling in the ballistic regime" is a quantum effect semiclassics misses; diffraction "is also expected to have major implications" for "apertures smaller than their Fermi wavelength"; semiclassics works where structures are "large compared to the Fermi wavelength"; they keep d/λ_F fixed (4.9, 6.8, ≈16) when scaling; complex absorbing potentials needed at edges to suppress hard-wall interference. — [arXiv:1805.10220](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.10220)
- Klein tunnelling / pn junctions: Chen et al. use T ∼ exp(−πk_F d sin²θ) (symmetric junction of width d); best fit d = 70 nm; Veselago focusing with 50% transmission relative to an abrupt junction "requires the d to be less than 5 nm". — [arXiv:1602.08182](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.08182)
- Phase coherence: Young & Kim observed Fabry–Pérot/Klein interference in graphene heterojunctions "at temperatures as high as 60 K", expected scale ħv_F/L ∼ 100 K for their junction length. — [arXiv:0808.0855](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0808.0855); suspended-graphene pn junctions show interference of electrons ballistic over >1 µm with visibility enhanced by Klein collimation (cryogenic). — [arXiv:1304.6590](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.6590); Taychatanapat: quantum-interference fine structure on TMF peaks washes out at ≈15 K. — [arXiv:1301.1969](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.1969); encapsulated bilayer weak-localization coherence times are sub-K to few-K measurements limited by e–e dephasing. — [arXiv:1403.1547](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1403.1547)
- Semiclassical vs quantum Veselago lensing in graphene: TB transmission curves are "in very good agreement with the results of a billiard model that incorporates trigonal warping" (1509.02370); semiclassical diffraction-catastrophe treatment of Veselago lensing (1703.07769). — [ar5iv 1509.02370](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1509.02370); [ar5iv 1703.07769](https://ar5iv.org/html/1703.07769)
- Geometric diodes with few-nm necks (4, 6, 8 nm) require atomistic DFTB; asymmetry 1.40 at |V_DS| = 0.2 V for a 4 nm neck (Mohebbi et al. 2024); Mencarelli et al. use self-consistent coherent TB scattering matrices for >10⁵-atom asymmetric graphene structures; drift-diffusion authors note atomistic methods are "impractical for simulating devices with dimensions exceeding a few nanometers". — [DOAJ Adv. Electron. Mater. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/4bd10bb4571448c382f1b1e72a72dbea); [IRIS UnivPM](https://iris.univpm.it/handle/11566/314272); [RSC Nanoscale Adv. 2024](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/na/d3na00603d)
- Experimental geometric-diode benchmark: asymmetry ratio 1.97 for a graphene diode with 23 and 26 nm necks (Wang et al. 2024, as cited). — [RSC Nanoscale Adv. 2024](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/na/d3na00603d)
- Trigonal warping affects TMF peak positions and is detectable in ballistic experiments (Fermi-surface anisotropy). — [arXiv:1901.06073](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.06073)

### Inferences
- Criteria for a ≈25 nm neck: (i) W/λ_F ≈ 0.7 at 10¹² cm⁻² (≈1 at 2×10¹²) → diffraction-dominated aperture, N ≈ 1–2 modes: classical ray counting through the neck is quantitatively wrong (it predicts a continuous G ∝ W, not a staircase, and ignores evanescent/tunnelling contributions); (ii) ΔE ≈ 83 meV ≫ k_BT(300 K) so quantization is not thermally averaged — but edge roughness of ±20% of W (Terrés) smears the staircase into kinks; (iii) coherence: L_T ≈ 25 nm at 300 K means interference between paths differing by more than ≈25 nm is averaged out at room temperature, so Fabry–Pérot resonances across a ≈100 nm-scale cavity will be invisible at 300 K but present at 4 K (L_T ≈ 2 µm, and measured coherence survives to 15–60 K). Hence at 300 K a semiclassical model with quantum-corrected neck transmission (e.g., a Landauer mode count for the neck, N(E) = ⌊k_F(E)W/π⌋ smoothed, as a transmission "aperture function") is a defensible hybrid; at 4 K a full quantum calculation is required for anything with features ≲100 nm.
- Rectification requires asymmetry in T(E) across the bias window or inelastic processes; for a 25 nm neck the energy dependence of N(E) (k_F ∝ E) is strong and genuinely quantum (mode opening), so the quantum model may predict rectification the classical billiard cannot; conversely the classical billiard only rectifies via momentum relaxation or self-consistent fields (B2). Any claim about a graphene Tesla-valve "diode" at the 25 nm scale should therefore be checked against Kwant/TB before trusting MC numbers.
- Kwant itself was used only as a benchmark in the sources found; no published Kwant study of a graphene Tesla valve or asymmetric-constriction rectifier compared with classical billiards was located (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No paper was found that directly compares a Kwant/TB simulation of a graphene ballistic rectifier or constriction against a classical billiard for the same geometry; the closest are Calogero (Veselago dots vs semiclassics) and 1509.02370 (Veselago lens TB vs billiard).
- Room-temperature phase-coherence length in hBN-encapsulated graphene was not found measured; only the L_T estimate above (researcher's) and low-T FP/TMF fine-structure temperatures.

## B6. Published comparisons between classical trajectory (event-driven/billiard) simulations and experiment for graphene nanostructures — where classical trajectories work; graphene Tesla-valve prior art

### Takeaway
Classical billiard/ray-tracing models have reproduced (i) scanning-gate images of cyclotron orbits and TMF (Bhandari et al.; diffuse edges needed), (ii) TMF/Veselago refraction peak positions and relative intensities in pn-junction devices (Chen et al., Science 2016, with a Klein transmission factor and smooth junction d = 70 nm), (iii) commensurability features in antidot lattices (Sandner et al. 2015; quasi-classical analysis by Datseris et al.), and (iv) Veselago-dot caustics (Bøggild et al.; confirmed by TB/NEGF). Classical models fail on interference fine structure (TMF fine structure, Auton's 50 K oscillations) and on hydrodynamic effects. For the Tesla-valve geometry specifically, the only graphene experiment (Geurs et al. 2020) found rectification only in the hydrodynamic window (D ≈ 1.05 for 20 K < T < 100 K at hole densities |n| > 5×10¹¹ cm⁻², micron-scale single loop), with none in the ballistic (2 K) or diffusive (110 K) regimes; a 2026 GaAs electron Tesla valve reports D up to ≈40 and calls the graphene result "a vanishing rectification of ∼5%".

### Cited Findings
- Bhandari et al.: "The ray-tracing method accurately describes images of cyclotron orbits in graphene taken by a cooled scanning probe microscope in the ballistic regime"; with diffuse edges the simulation reproduces both the reduced transmission along the cyclotron orbit and the enhanced transmission when the tip deflects electrons away "from the diffusely scattering edge"; proposed improvement: include the fringing-field density divergence at edges. — [arXiv:1608.02248](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.02248)
- Chen et al. (Science 2016): "Excellent agreement is found between the peak positions and the theoretical curves for all four cases" (Snell's law with k_i = √(πn_i) as index; negative-unity slope confirms negative refraction in the pn′ case); the billiard simulation "matches well with the general features of our experimental data for both pp′ and pn′ cases"; the angular transmission matched d = 70 nm and deviated from the abrupt-junction curve; the device is 3.9 µm × 3.9 µm, signal vanishes at ≈70 K. — [arXiv:1602.08182](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1602.08182)
- Taychatanapat et al.: TMF peak positions follow the classical cyclotron condition, with 3 orders visible in MLG; interference fine structure (not classical) disappears above ≈15 K. — [arXiv:1301.1969](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.1969)
- Sandner et al. (Nano Lett. 15, 2015): hBN-encapsulated graphene antidot lattices with periods down to 50 nm show commensurability features from ballistic orbits around 1, 2 or 4 antidots. — [Regensburg eprint](https://pred.uni-regensburg.de/id/eprint/4381/); Datseris, Geisel, Fleischmann give a quasi-classical explanation linking the peaks to collision times and phase-space volumes. — [arXiv:1711.05833](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05833); a fully atomistic device-scale simulation reports "excellent agreement with recent experimental results" for these peaks. — [arXiv:1703.05665](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/1703.05665v2)
- Calogero et al.: TB/NEGF confirms the semiclassical caustic positions of Bøggild et al.'s Veselago dots (main cusp), with quantum broadening. — [arXiv:1805.10220](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1805.10220)
- Auton et al. (graphene ballistic nano-rectifier): a "simplistic billiard ball model" with current-induced collimation explains rectification; low-power output linear "as expected from the extended Büttiker–Landauer formalism"; the billiard does not explain the 50 K oscillations (quantization) or the low-T output reversal (Seebeck). Responsivity 23,000 mV/mW. — [PMC4895026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4895026/)
- Z-shaped geometric-diode PIC-MC: an inverse-arrowhead diode "was fabricated and measured with results showing correspondence to the simulator" (Wang et al.); asymmetry peaks near λ_MFP ≈ 600 nm for a 100 nm neck. — [PMC8470659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8470659/)
- Geurs et al. (2020, hBN-encapsulated graphene Tesla valve, single loop, micron-sized leads): "Rectification occurs at carrier densities and temperatures consistent with the hydrodynamic regime" and "disappears both in the ballistic and diffusive transport regimes"; diodicity D = R_f/R_b with "one area with D≈1.05" for 20 K < T < 100 K and n < −5×10¹¹ cm⁻² (Device 1); near charge neutrality a large bias-induced, temperature-independent rectification is not viscous (explained by a transistor model with shape factor K = 4.5); at 110 K "the device acts as an Ohmic resistor"; at 2 K no viscous rectification; ballistic rectifiers are said to need "multiple submicron orifices or quantum point contacts"; the fluid simulation shown is a COMSOL model of a mechanical Tesla valve; lumped-element rectification ratio "does not depend on the number of loops". — [arXiv:2008.04862](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.04862v1)
- GaAs electron Tesla valve (2026 preprint, [single paper]): "the only study in graphene reports a vanishing rectification of ∼5%"; channel widths 2.5 and 5 µm, l_MR > 10 µm below 20 K, l_ee < 0.5 µm at I = 350 µA (T_e ≈ 100 K); diodicity "as high as Di≈40 in wider valve", "more than tenfold difference between forward and reverse resistances"; rectification "almost vanished at T = 70 K"; mechanism argued to be hydrodynamic/turbulent (Re ≳ 7); no trajectory, MC or Boltzmann simulation and no edge-specularity statement. — [arXiv:2603.16443](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16443v1)
- Hydrodynamic model of a graphene Tesla valve (Materials 2022): unipolar hydrodynamic (continuity/momentum/energy with relaxation terms, not Navier–Stokes with explicit viscosity), zero-velocity ghost-cell edges, narrow width "in the order of 10 nm", room temperature, momentum-relaxation time 0.27 ps at E_F = 100 meV; predicted diodicity D = 1.0655; "Higher diodicities can be easily achieved by cascading"; only qualitative comparison to Geurs. — [PMC9228317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9228317/)
- Truccolo et al. (2022): MC + Landauer–Büttiker framework for ballistic graphene rectifiers exploiting long mean free path (abstract only). — [AIR UniUD](https://air.uniud.it/handle/11390/1226027)

### Inferences
- Where classical trajectories demonstrably work: feature sizes ≳ 0.5–1 µm (contacts ≥ 100 nm), fields where r_c ≫ λ_F, T where coherence is lost (≳ 15–60 K) but l_m still exceeds the path (≲ 70–300 K depending on path length), and observables that are angle-/position-integrated (peak positions, transmission maps). Where they fail: sub-100 nm necks (mode quantization, Auton), interference fine structure, Klein tunnelling (needs an explicit angle-dependent transmission factor), and hydrodynamic (e–e) effects.
- For a ballistic graphene Tesla valve, the existing experiment indicates that a non-interacting billiard should predict essentially zero rectification at micron scale (consistent with the Landauer reciprocity argument in B2); any predicted ballistic rectification in a simulator must be traced to momentum relaxation, self-consistent fields or diffuse-wall asymmetry, and should be cross-checked against the "zero at V → 0, specular walls, no scattering" limit.
- The GaAs result (D ≈ 40 at W = 2.5–5 µm with l_MR > 10 µm > W) is a single preprint whose claimed hydrodynamic mechanism is not independently simulated; it is a motivating data point, not a validated benchmark.

### Gaps
- Geurs et al.'s exact channel widths, loop dimensions and whether any ballistic (billiard) simulation was attempted were not stated in the retrieved text.
- No published event-driven MC of a graphene Tesla valve in the ballistic regime was found; Truccolo et al.'s framework (closest methodological match) was accessible only as an abstract.
